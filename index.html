NEO SOUL

Co-founder · 09/2016–01/2020

NEO SOUL Entertainment · A pioneer in bringing East Asian live music to North America. Los Angeles.

The most volatile industry. The fiercest competition. I started a company there anyway.

The gap

Chinese students in the US had little to go out for. Chinese indie music was booming back home. Nobody connected the two.

The move

We brought the artists to US stages. With no market data, I built the forecasting and pricing models to pick venues, size cities and price tickets, and led an 11-person team from planning to show day.

The result

19 concerts, forecasts within 5%. US$600K in revenue and US$250K raised across 2 rounds, at a US$6.3M angel-round valuation. We paused in early 2020, when our artists could no longer travel out of China.

How I worked it

Forecasting a tour with no history

Our artists had never toured the Americas, and we still had to choose cities, venues, capacity and prices. I combined streaming data city by city, social data on who the fans were, and box office, capacity and sell-through from similar artists who had toured here. Put together in Excel Power Query, they gave a box office forecast for each city.

US$6.3M

Angel-round valuation

Valuation set at the first angel round. Both rounds, US$250K in total, came from the same angel investor.

SparkLease

Product Marketing Manager · 01/2026–09/2026 · Government-funded contract

SparkLease · Car leasing and deals marketplace with 100,000+ annual active users.

Carmakers didn’t need another website. They needed shelf space.

The gap

100,000+ active users a year, and nothing packaged for the carmakers who wanted to reach them.

The move

I grew organic leads with SEO and AI search, then turned the site’s screen space into a tiered product for OEMs and dealers.

The result

Ranking keywords grew about 20-fold during my term, with top-5 Google rankings across 5 target keyword groups and more organic leads from the same traffic. 7 cold pitches to BMW’s agency of record, Mercedes-Benz and GM brands; 3 shortlisted.

How I worked it

Shelf space, not another website

People came for lease takeovers and deals. They were all about to change cars, and none had picked a model yet. That is the last step before a purchase, and the one carmakers most want to reach.

So I packaged the screen space for carmakers and their dealers, set it apart from media that only sell exposure, and made sales material their agencies could take straight to their carmaker clients.

Bilingual, because most users read English

A German premium carmaker’s new agency wanted to beat its predecessor’s results on our platform. About 85% of our users read in English, so a Chinese-only campaign would miss most of them. I proposed bilingual creative and 4 tracked entry points on the site, each leading to the carmaker’s landing pages.

Finding data

When the numbers don’t exist yet, I go find where they must be.

Insurance and wealth

The data had to exist

  1. An insurance and wealth company asked which first-generation immigrant communities invest, and how much. No one had published that cut.

  2. Almost everyone in Canada files a tax return, and every return has a line for investment income. So the data had to exist. Statistics Canada publishes it by ethnic group: free, official and complete.

  3. I put 2 questions on 1 chart. Across: the share of people with any investment income, or how many invest. Up: the median amount, or how much. Chinese Canadians sat furthest out on both, at about 2 times the national median.

    A median of about $1,400 a year looks small. But an investment firm cares about the money behind the income, not the income itself. Working back from typical returns turned a small number into a large market.

Baby care

1 chart, 3 answers

  1. A diaper brand asked which community was its biggest opportunity. I knew nothing about baby products, and there was no report to buy.

  2. A classmate who had just become a father mentioned the Canada Child Benefit. If families claim it, Statistics Canada counts it, and it splits the count by ethnic group.

  3. 1 bubble chart answered 3 questions. Across: the share of people receiving the benefit, or how likely a household is to have young children. Up: the median amount, which rises with the number of children. Bubble size: how many people receive it, or how big the market is.

    Arab Canadians sat furthest out on both axes. The community is large, and one language links people from many countries, so it can be reached as one audience. I left income out on purpose. Diapers are bought at every income.

Consumer electronics

Not a conversion problem

  1. An electronics brand wanted more people to reach its product pages through its WeChat account. The content was good, and the brand liked it. Traffic still stayed low.

  2. I took the account apart by media type: owned, earned and paid. The content and the path to the product pages held up. Nothing paid was sending people to the account.

  3. Hardly anyone in Canada knew the account existed. It was a discovery problem, not a conversion problem. Fix the way in, and conversion follows.

Skincare

An anti-aging first step

  1. A skincare brand was launching an oat-based anti-aging line and asked how to position it for Chinese Canadians, and who to talk to.

  2. At the product first, then at who buys the brand. Census data showed Chinese Canadian birth rates below the national average, so talking only to parents would miss most of the audience. Reviews and consumer research showed many young adults, especially those with sensitive skin, already using the brand’s baby line on themselves.

  3. For this audience, the brand’s equity was its baby line, and “safe for a baby” meant “safe for me.” The same oat ingredient runs through the baby line and the new product, so the link was already there.

    The audience was people who hadn’t started anti-aging yet, or had tried retinol and couldn’t take it. Starting anti-aging is an anxious purchase, not an exciting one. So the line was an entry, not an upgrade: a first anti-aging step, without retinol’s irritation, from a brand they already trusted with babies.

Chapter 03

Reading the audience

Start from what people already do, not from the brand.

Household cleaning

A holiday in 3 cleans

  1. A household cleaning brand had little recognition among first-generation Chinese Canadians. How does a brand people don’t know get into their homes?

  2. Not at the brand. At the calendar. Chinese New Year is when Chinese families clean the most, and the cleaning follows rules.

  3. The holiday has 3 cleans, not 1. Before: from Little New Year the whole house is cleaned, and sweeping the dust sounds like sweeping out the old year. During: from day 1 to day 5, no sweeping and no taking out the trash, so the luck stays in. These are also the days with the most guests and the most food. After: once day 5 passes, the house is cleaned again.

    Each clean needs something different. And a brand that shows up inside a ritual doesn’t have to introduce itself first.

Watches

Luxury’s core buyer

  1. An entry-level Swiss luxury watch brand wanted to bring a campaign fronted by a Chinese actress to Chinese Canadians.

  2. At who buys luxury watches. Bain and broker reports put China among the world’s largest luxury watch markets.

  3. That habit travels with first-generation immigrants and international students. So in Canada, Chinese buyers are a core group for luxury watches across every brand. An entry-level price speaks to younger buyers, and the ambassador sat right in that sweet spot.

    Media followed the audience’s habits, in Chinese and English, with QR codes straight to the brand’s landing page.

Home furnishings

The question nobody prepped for

  1. In the pitch room, the client asked: if a regional offer spills outside the area, people see a deal they can’t use. What then? No one had an answer ready.

  2. At what was really being asked. The worry was how people would react, and no one fully controls that.

  3. So I answered only the parts we could control. Media: geofenced to the eligible area. Creative: a clear line on where the offer applies, so even a shared ad doesn’t mislead.

Financial services

A week’s RFP in 2.5 days

  1. A major financial institution sent an RFP with a heavy scope. We had 3 days. Other bidders had a week.

  2. At who could do what, fastest.

  3. I set up a Kanban board, split the work into tasks with SMART goals by expertise, and mapped roles in a RACI matrix. A kickoff walked the team through the key requirements, and I handled issues as they came up.

Automotive

The brand work was already done

  1. An American carmaker’s 2 brands had little pull in Canada. One wasn’t popular locally. The other had no premium image here.

  2. At where first-generation Chinese immigrants first learned about cars: mainland China.

  3. There, the first brand sells more than anywhere else in the world, so the goodwill already exists. The second mostly arrives as parallel imports: expensive, scarce, and seen as premium.

    The brand education had already happened in China. The carmaker could skip it, and get a different perception from the one it has with other Canadians.

Connected car services

Safety first

  1. A connected safety service wanted more paying subscribers.

  2. At what Chinese Canadian families value, and at our own listings.

  3. Family comes first, so safety comes first. A safety service means more to this audience than to most: after a crash, it is the first voice you hear.

    Our platform also listed many recent used cars from the same carmaker, the exact cars the service runs on. A free trial on those cars could lead to paid subscriptions.

Premium automotive · Via a partner agency

Quiet luxury, proven in winter

  1. A premium carmaker wanted stronger brand equity among Chinese Canadians, around a leasing offer and a Mid-Autumn campaign.

  2. At the people before the car.

  3. This audience prefers luxury that is understated and well made, and shows status through quality, not display. That matched the brand’s global position, but no one had made the link for them.

    I tied all-wheel drive to Canadian winters, as proof of reliability, which this audience weighs heavily in a big purchase.

Consumer goods loyalty program

Value, in their own language

  1. A consumer goods loyalty program wanted to reach Chinese Canadians, who knew neither the parent company nor the program.

  2. At how this audience shops. I built a persona from it: careful with money, not willing to pay for a label, loyal once convinced, open to new brands, and attentive to whether a brand respects their culture.

  3. A buyer who hunts for value meets a program built on value and rewards. The fit was already there.

    Chinese New Year is a shopping holiday, and WeChat is the community’s main native-language platform. Those two were the way in.
